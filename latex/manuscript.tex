\begin{abstract}
More than sixty emerging and developing economies have adopted green banking guidelines through the Sustainable Banking and Finance Network (SBFN). The policy's theory of change holds that supervisory guidelines lead banks to price environmental risk, so that credit flows to firms investing in cleaner production. We evaluate two links in that chain. At firm level, using two independent World Bank Enterprise Survey samples (28{,}042 firms in 41 economies; 89{,}797 firms in 162 economies) and four estimators, bank credit is associated with 13-percentage-point higher green-practice adoption, but the association is the same in adopting and non-adopting economies (0.076 versus 0.072 in a causal forest) and does not vary with regulatory quality. At country level, a staggered-adoption event study of 217 economies finds no effect on renewable-energy share, and a CO2 association that appears to reflect which economies join. The guidelines show no detectable effect on the margin they target. For policy making, the results suggest that green finance programmes should be judged on firm and environmental outcomes rather than on guideline adoption alone, and that guidelines aimed at banks may need complements aimed at firms, such as technical assistance or credit guarantees.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Since 2012, more than sixty central banks and financial regulators in emerging and transition economies have joined the IFC-hosted Sustainable Banking and Finance Network (SBFN), issuing guidelines that direct commercial banks to price environmental risk into lending and to channel credit towards cleaner production. The network monitors this effort through policy outputs: which guidelines each member has issued, and how far it has moved along the network's own progression framework \citep{ifc2025sbfn}. It does not monitor whether firms change what they do. Whether a firm that can obtain a bank loan is any more likely to install pollution-control equipment or measure its own emissions because such a guideline exists is the question an evaluation of the policy has to answer.

We treat SBFN adoption as a policy intervention and evaluate it against its own theory of change (Section~\ref{sec:toc}). That theory runs from a supervisory guideline, through banks pricing environmental risk and reallocating credit, to financed firms investing more in cleaner production, and from there to better aggregate environmental outcomes. If the chain works, two things should be observable: the link between bank credit and firms' green investment should be stronger where a guideline is in force, and adopting economies' environmental indicators should improve after adoption. We test both.

Country-level studies find that green-finance policy works better where governance is stronger \citep{chi2023,jia2026,shi2024,ullahw2024}, and firm-level studies find that financing constraints shape green investment \citep{costa2024,dehaas2025,ghosh2022}. Neither literature has tested, on firm-level data and across a broad cross-section of adopting economies, whether green banking guidelines strengthen the credit-to-green channel they are designed to activate.

We use two independently assembled World Bank Enterprise Survey (WBES) samples: a 41-economy primary sample of 28{,}042 firms surveyed in 2018--2020 with the Bank's full Green Economy module, and a 162-economy global sample of 89{,}797 firms surveyed in 2021--2026 that extends to high-income economies outside the SBFN's remit. Because SBFN status is constant within a country in a single survey wave, we combine a pooled logit benchmark with a Bayesian hierarchical model, a causal forest, and, on the larger sample, country fixed effects. Across all four estimators the answer is the same, and it is precise: in the causal forest the credit effect is 0.076 in adopting economies and 0.072 in non-adopting ones. A Callaway--Sant'Anna staggered-adoption event study of 217 economies then tests the aggregate outcome directly.

The paper contributes to the evaluation of green finance policy in four ways. It evaluates SBFN-style adoption against firm-level outcomes rather than against the policy-stage indicators the network reports or the bank-level lending data that dominate the green-credit literature. It tests the theory of change at two points, with different data, units of analysis and identification strategies. It replicates the firm-level result on an independent sample with a different outcome and country fixed effects. And because the estimates are precise rather than merely insignificant, it can report a genuine absence of effect on the targeted margin, the kind of null result that matters for programme design but is rarely published.

Section~\ref{sec:institutions} describes the SBFN framework and its theory of change. Section~\ref{sec:literature} develops the hypotheses, Section~\ref{sec:methodology} sets out data and methods, Section~\ref{sec:results} evaluates the firm-level credit channel and Section~\ref{sec:macrodid} the aggregate outcome. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} draws lessons for policy and evaluation, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Green banking policy and its theory of change}
\label{sec:institutions}

The SBFN, founded in 2012 as the Sustainable Banking Network and hosted by the International Finance Corporation, is the main forum through which financial regulators and banking associations in emerging and developing economies coordinate national sustainable-finance policy. Its roughly seventy member institutions come almost entirely from Africa, Asia, Latin America, the Middle East and non-EU Europe \citep{ifc2025sbfn}; none of the sixteen EU member states in our primary sample is a member.

Membership is the network's coarsest measure of adoption. Its Multi-Tiered Progress Framework places each member on a progression from `Commitment' or `Formulating', through `Developing', to `Advancing' and `Consolidating'. Our sample spans that whole range: Mongolia and Morocco had reached `Advancing' by their 2019 surveys, whereas Jordan and the Kyrgyz Republic were still at `Commitment'. Several economies, including Kazakhstan, Serbia, Ukraine and most of the Western Balkans, joined only after their survey was fielded. We code these as non-adopters, because what matters is the policy environment in force when a firm answered the survey.

\subsection{The policy's theory of change and what this evaluation tests}
\label{sec:toc}

The case that SBFN adoption should change firm behaviour rests on a supervisory mechanism with five links:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Adoption.} A banking regulator or banking association issues environmental-and-social risk management guidelines.
\item \emph{Bank response.} Supervised banks face a compliance incentive to price environmental risk into lending and, in more advanced frameworks, to report a green-lending share.
\item \emph{Credit allocation.} Credit flows more readily to firms undertaking cleaner investment and less readily to heavy polluters.
\item \emph{Firm response.} Firms with bank credit invest more in green practices than they would without the guideline, so the credit-to-green link is stronger where the guideline is in force (H2), and stronger still where the supervisor can enforce it (H3).
\item \emph{Aggregate outcome.} Adopting economies' environmental indicators improve relative to non-adopters after adoption.
\end{enumerate}

Evidence on China's green credit guidelines documents links 2 to 4 at bank and firm level \citep{dai2025,gao2022,huang2023,li2023}, and studies of Bangladesh and Vietnam point the same way \citep{khairunnessa2021,nguyen2025}. Whether the chain holds across a heterogeneous set of SBFN adopters is untested. This paper tests link 4 at firm level (Section~\ref{sec:results}) and link 5 at country level (Section~\ref{sec:macrodid}). It does not observe links 2 and 3: the Enterprise Surveys record whether a firm holds bank credit, not the terms banks offered or the green share of their lending. A null at link 4 therefore shows that the chain does not deliver its intended firm-level effect, but cannot on its own say where it breaks.

\section{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:literature}

Green investments such as energy-management systems, on-site renewable generation and waste-minimisation retrofits carry a large up-front cost against savings realised over several years, the profile of investment most sensitive to external finance. Firm-level evidence is consistent with this. Credit-constrained Indian manufacturers are less likely to adopt cleaner production \citep{ghosh2022}; financial and managerial barriers jointly constrain green investment across countries \citep{dehaas2025,kalantzis2022}; financing constraints blunt firms' response to environmental policy in OECD and Vietnamese data \citep{costa2024,truong2026}; and firms with better credit access sustain green management through shocks \citep{aristei2023}. The WBES Green Economy module has been used to study such behaviour across many countries \citep{asif2025,phan2026}. The first-order proposition is:

\begin{quotation}
\noindent\textbf{H1.} Firms with access to formal bank credit (a credit line or loan) are more likely to have adopted green production practices than otherwise similar firms without it.
\end{quotation}

H1 describes a channel, not a policy. The premise of SBFN-style guidelines is that a supervisory mandate should widen the gap in green investment between firms with and without bank credit, because banks in adopting jurisdictions have an incentive to seek out and price favourably exactly the borrowers making such investments. Most supporting evidence comes from single-country studies of China's unusually well-resourced regime \citep{dai2025,gao2022}. Whether the same moderation holds across the far more heterogeneous, and often less advanced, set of SBFN adopters is untested:

\begin{quotation}
\noindent\textbf{H2.} The association between bank credit and green-practice adoption (H1) is stronger in economies that had adopted an SBFN framework by the survey year than in economies that had not.
\end{quotation}

A guideline is only as effective as the supervisor behind it. The law-and-finance and state-capacity literatures show that formally identical rules produce different outcomes depending on the institutions that enforce them \citep{acemoglu2001,laporta1998,levine2005}. Where examination authority, sanctions and disclosure enforcement are weak, an SBFN framework may exist on paper without changing lending. Country-level evidence fits this view: green finance improves environmental outcomes only where corruption is controlled \citep{shi2024}, China's guidelines work best where environmental governance is strong \citep{chi2023}, and governance strengthens the green-credit-to-sustainable-production link in BRICS economies \citep{jia2026}. Each result implies an untested firm-level prediction. Proxying regulatory capacity by the Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) Regulatory Quality estimate, we hypothesise a three-way interaction:

\begin{quotation}
\noindent\textbf{H3.} The moderation in H2 increases with regulatory quality: SBFN adoption strengthens the credit-to-green channel most where the supervisor has the capacity to make the guideline credible.
\end{quotation}

\subsection{An auxiliary, exploratory hypothesis}
\label{sec:h4}

Financial constraints bind environmental investment most tightly in smaller firms \citep{ullah2025}, consistent with a fixed cost that is proportionately heavier for them even once financed. We did not pre-specify a firm-size hypothesis. It emerged from the causal forest (Section~\ref{sec:causalforest}), and we report it as exploratory:

\begin{quotation}
\noindent\textbf{H4 (exploratory).} Heterogeneity in the credit-to-green channel is more attributable to firm size than to either country-level moderator.
\end{quotation}

\section{Data and methods}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Data}

The WBES are stratified random-sample surveys of formal, non-agricultural private firms with at least five employees. Our primary sample is the Bank's harmonised file for 41 Europe and Central Asia and Middle East and North Africa economies surveyed with an identical questionnaire in 2018--2020, the wave in which the Green Economy module was fielded in full: 28{,}042 firms (Supplementary Table~S2). Six further economies (Bangladesh 2022, India 2022, Indonesia 2023, Peru 2023, the Philippines 2023 and Timor-Leste 2021; 15{,}556 firms) fielded a shortened, partly randomised version of the module. Because item wording differs, we do not pool them, and use them only for a narrower check (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).

The global sample comes from the Bank's standardised cross-country database, which harmonises core variables across 471 country-years and 292{,}314 firms. In it, 160 country-years fielded in 2021--2026, including high-income economies such as Australia, Canada, Germany and Japan, carry CO2-emissions monitoring alongside the standard access-to-finance questions. Adding Bangladesh 2022 and Indonesia 2023 from our own extraction of the country files gives 162 economies and 100{,}115 firms, of which 89{,}797 have complete data. Controls come from the Bank's Productivity Database, merged by firm identifier.

SBFN status, join year and progression stage for the primary sample are compiled from the network's Global Progress Reports (2018, 2019, 2021, 2023--2024), country addenda and data portal, cross-checked against IFC accession announcements. For the global sample we use the portal's member roster (67 members). In both, an economy counts as an adopter only if it joined before its survey year. Six CEMAC economies and four Eastern Caribbean economies appear on the roster under regional entries, which we read as covering their common banking supervisors; these are inferences rather than per-country listings. Regulatory quality is the WGI estimate for the survey year (or the latest earlier year); WGI does not cover Taiwan, which is dropped.

A staggered difference-in-differences design around each economy's join date is not feasible at firm level. The Green Economy module has been fielded to most economies only once, and economies surveyed twice by the core instrument did not repeat a comparable module, so no consistent outcome is observed before and after adoption. We therefore run the staggered design at country level (Section~\ref{sec:macrodid}) and treat the firm-level cross-section as a test of link 4.

\subsection{Variables}

The outcome, \emph{green practice adoption}, equals one if a firm adopted at least one of seven practices in the previous three years: climate-friendly on-site energy generation, an energy-management system, waste minimisation or recycling, air-pollution control, other pollution control, any energy-efficiency measure, or on-site renewable energy. The mean of the seven items serves as a continuous index for robustness. \emph{Bank credit} equals one if the firm holds a credit line or loan from a formal financial institution. \emph{SBFN member} equals one if the economy had adopted an SBFN framework by the survey year, and \emph{regulatory quality} is the WGI estimate, standardised within the sample for the hierarchical and machine-learning models. Controls are firm size (small, medium or large), broad sector, log sales, exporter status and any foreign ownership. Supplementary Table~S1 gives the WBES code and source of every variable.

\subsection{Estimation}

\textbf{Stage 1: pooled logit benchmark.} We estimate
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{Green}_{i,c}=1) = \Lambda\Big(\beta_1 \text{Credit}_{i,c} + \beta_2 \text{SBFN}_c + \beta_3 (\text{Credit}_{i,c}\times \text{SBFN}_c) + \mathbf{X}_{i,c}'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + \delta_{s(i)}\Big)
\end{equation}
for firm $i$ in country $c$, with firm controls $\mathbf{X}_{i,c}$, sector fixed effects $\delta_{s(i)}$ and country-clustered standard errors. A country fixed effect would absorb $\text{SBFN}_c$, so $\beta_2$ would be unidentified. The interaction $\beta_3$ would remain identified, because credit varies within countries, but only from the contrast between eight adopter economies and thirty-three non-adopters, and the level difference between them would be lost. We therefore estimate $\beta_2$ and $\beta_3$ without country fixed effects, and add a triple interaction with regulatory quality to test H3 directly (column M3).

\textbf{Stage 2: Bayesian hierarchical model.} Country enters as a random effect, with a random slope on credit:
\begin{align}
\text{Green}_{i,c} &\sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_{i,c}) \\
\text{logit}(p_{i,c}) &= (\beta_1 + u_{1,c})\,\text{Credit}_{i,c} + \beta_2 \text{SBFN}_c + \beta_3(\text{Credit}_{i,c}\times \text{SBFN}_c) \nonumber \\
&\quad + \beta_4 (\text{Credit}_{i,c}\times \text{RegQuality}_c) + \mathbf{X}_{i,c}'\boldsymbol{\gamma} + (\alpha + u_{0,c}) \\
\begin{pmatrix} u_{0,c} \\ u_{1,c} \end{pmatrix} &\sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}\right)
\end{align}
estimated by the No-U-Turn Sampler in PyMC \citep{salvatier2016}. Partial pooling keeps the country-level main effects identified while giving more defensible inference than cluster-robust standard errors over only 41 clusters \citep{cameron2015,gelman2007}. $\beta_3$ tests H2; $\beta_4$ tests whether regulatory quality alone moderates the credit-green link. Neither is a test of H3, which needs a three-way term that eight adopter economies cannot support under partial pooling. H3 is tested by Stage~1's column M3 and by the saturated global-sample model.

\textbf{Stage 3: causal forest.} To avoid imposing a linear interaction, we estimate a causal forest with double machine learning \citep{athey2019grf,chernozhukov2018,wager2018}, using gradient-boosted nuisance models and firm size, export status, SBFN status and regulatory quality as effect modifiers. Its identifying assumption is the same unconfoundedness on observables as Stages 1 and 2, so its conditional average treatment effects (CATEs) are flexible conditional associations, not effects identified from exogenous variation.

\textbf{Stage 4: global sample.} We re-estimate the model on the global sample with CO2-emissions monitoring as the outcome. With 159 country clusters, country fixed effects become usable: they absorb the SBFN and regulatory-quality main effects but leave their interactions with credit identified. We report this alongside a saturated specification without fixed effects that retains the three-way term.

\section{Evaluating the firm-level credit channel}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Descriptive patterns}
\label{sec:descriptive}

Firm counts in the primary sample range from 150 (Montenegro) to 3{,}075 (Egypt), and the unconditional adoption rate from 30.3\% (Egypt) to 90.1\% (Malta). Eight of the 41 economies had adopted an SBFN framework by their survey year: Egypt, Georgia, Jordan, the Kyrgyz Republic, Mongolia, Morocco, Tunisia and Türkiye (Figure~\ref{fig:map}). Bank credit correlates with the adoption index ($r=0.18$) but not with firms' perceived finance obstacle ($r=-0.02$). Figure~\ref{fig:scatter} previews the main result. Plotting adoption against regulatory quality separately for members and non-members gives two lines of similar slope about 15--20 percentage points apart: a level difference, with no difference in slope.

\subsection{Pooled logit benchmark}
\label{sec:baseline}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} supports H1. Firms with a bank credit line are more likely to have adopted a green practice ($b=0.571$, $p<0.001$), with an average marginal effect of 12.95 percentage points (95\% CI [7.7, 18.2]), about the same as moving from a small to a large firm. Column M2 shows a large negative SBFN main effect ($b=-0.991$, $p<0.001$) and a small, insignificant credit $\times$ SBFN interaction ($b=0.049$, $p=0.773$), contrary to H2. Column M3 tests H3: the credit $\times$ SBFN $\times$ regulatory-quality term is small, of the wrong sign and insignificant ($b=-0.104$, $\text{se}=0.272$, $p=0.702$). Regulatory quality's own association with adoption is positive but imprecise ($b=0.278$, $p=0.110$).

\subsection{Bayesian hierarchical model}
\label{sec:multilevel}

The hierarchical model (Table~\ref{tab:multilevel}; four chains, all $\hat{R}\leq1.01$, bulk effective sample sizes 797--2{,}270; Supplementary Section~S7) gives H2 a fair hearing without relying on 41-cluster standard errors. The credit effect survives (posterior mean 0.33, 89\% interval [0.18, 0.49]). SBFN membership lowers baseline adoption ($-0.57$, [$-1.00$, $-0.09$]) and regulatory quality raises it (0.34, [0.16, 0.53]), reproducing the level pattern in Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}. Neither interaction differs from zero: credit $\times$ SBFN is 0.072 ([$-0.26$, 0.40]) and credit $\times$ regulatory quality is 0.004 ([$-0.13$, 0.14]), a tight interval around zero rather than merely a wide one.

\subsection{Causal forest}
\label{sec:causalforest}

The causal forest (Table~\ref{tab:causalforest}, Figure~\ref{fig:cateplot}) imposes no functional form on moderation. Its average effect of credit is 0.073 (95\% CI [$-0.040$, 0.186]), smaller and less precise than the logit marginal effect, as expected of a cross-fitted, doubly robust estimator. The CATEs are flat across every institutional cut: 0.072 in non-adopting and 0.076 in adopting economies, and 0.075, 0.070 and 0.075 across the low, middle and high regulatory-quality terciles.

Firm size accounts for 75.6\% of the forest's explained heterogeneity, against 19.9\% for regulatory quality, 2.6\% for SBFN status and 1.9\% for exporter status, consistent with H4. But the size strata do not show a monotonic gradient (0.080 for small, 0.075 for medium and 0.057 for large firms, all with overlapping intervals that include zero; Supplementary Table~S3). We can say that heterogeneity sits at firm level rather than with country institutions, but not in which direction it runs.

\subsection{Robustness}
\label{sec:robustness}
\label{sec:extension}

The result holds with the continuous adoption index estimated by OLS, and separately for manufacturing and services (Supplementary Table~S5). In four of the six supplementary economies, an overdraft facility is associated with higher waste-minimisation adoption ($b=0.422$, $p<0.001$, $N=9{,}038$; Supplementary Table~S4), so the credit channel is not specific to the primary region. One interaction is nominally significant: with overdraft access in place of a credit line, the overdraft $\times$ SBFN term is negative ($b=-0.346$, $p=0.031$), the opposite of H2's prediction. It is one of thirteen interaction tests under frequentist estimators, and after a Benjamini--Hochberg correction its $p$-value rises to 0.403; no test in the family survives at a false discovery rate of 0.10 (Supplementary Table~S6).

\subsection{Replication on the global sample}
\label{sec:global}

The global sample differs from the primary one in outcome, period and economies, with little overlap (Figure~\ref{fig:globalmap}). Without country fixed effects (Table~\ref{tab:global}, M1), credit is associated with emissions monitoring ($b=0.198$, $p=0.039$; 89{,}797 firms, 159 countries), while the SBFN main effect ($b=-0.171$, $p=0.353$), the credit $\times$ SBFN interaction ($b=0.278$, $p=0.430$) and the regulatory-quality interactions are insignificant, including the three-way term this saturated model retains. With country fixed effects (M2), credit is more precisely estimated ($b=0.249$, $p<0.001$), and neither the credit $\times$ SBFN ($b=0.506$, $p=0.205$) nor the credit $\times$ regulatory-quality interaction ($b=-0.044$, $p=0.346$) is significant. Figure~\ref{fig:coefplot} collects every estimate. The credit effect is positive in both samples and under every estimator, and every interaction interval includes zero.

\section{Evaluating the aggregate outcome: a staggered-adoption event study}
\label{sec:macrodid}

The firm-level evidence addresses link 4. Link 5, the aggregate outcome the policy is ultimately meant to move, can be tested directly using SBFN's staggered join dates, because country-level outcomes are observed every year.

\subsection{Data and design}
\label{sec:macrodidmethod}

We build a country-year panel of 217 economies for 2000--2024. Treatment timing is each economy's SBFN join year from the portal roster, which agrees with our global-sample coding for all 61 overlapping economies. This gives 68 ever-treated economies (join years 2012--2024) and 149 never-treated ones. Outcomes from the World Development Indicators are renewable energy as a share of final energy consumption and CO2 emissions per capita; log GDP per capita (constant 2015 US dollars) is a covariate, because adopters are disproportionately fast-growing, industrialising economies.

We use the \citet{callaway2021} estimator, which estimates group-time average treatment effects by adoption cohort and aggregates them into an overall average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) and an event-study profile, with never-treated economies as controls and 999 bootstrap replications. Two-way fixed effects are biased under staggered adoption \citep{goodmanbacon2021}, so we report them only to show the size of that bias. For each outcome we estimate an unconditional and a doubly robust specification with log GDP per capita, and check the latter against a not-yet-treated control group.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:macrodidresults}

Renewable-energy share shows no effect (Table~\ref{tab:macrodid}): $-1.192$ (95\% CI [$-2.763$, 0.380]) unconditionally and 0.674 ([$-1.122$, 2.470]) with the GDP control, and the event-study profile is flat on both sides of adoption (Figure~\ref{fig:macroevent}). CO2 emissions per capita show a positive association, but three features argue against a causal reading. It falls by about 45\%, from 0.631 to 0.349, once GDP per capita is controlled for. Its event-study profile rises steadily from near zero at adoption to a peak nine to twelve years later, which looks like adopters already being on a steeper emissions path rather than like a policy effect. And two-way fixed effects give 0.901, more than double the doubly robust estimate. Both doubly robust results are essentially unchanged with not-yet-treated controls (0.313 for CO2 and 0.653 for renewables). The design finds no evidence that adoption improves an economy's environmental trajectory, and its one significant result is better explained by which economies choose to join.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

In plain terms, a firm with a bank credit line is about 13 percentage points more likely to have adopted a green practice than a similar firm without one. This is an association, not a causal effect. The gap is the same whether or not the firm's country has adopted an SBFN framework, and at every level of regulatory quality. Firms in adopting economies are instead 15--20 percentage points less likely to have adopted any green practice at all: a difference in level, not in slope. Because the two firm-level samples share few economies, periods or outcome items, and the event study uses different data, units and identification, this convergence is unlikely to be an artefact of one sample or estimator.

The result complicates a green-credit literature built largely on China's mature guidelines \citep{dai2025,huang2023,li2023}. Our adopters span every stage of SBFN's progression framework, and across that range we find no sign that formal adoption changes how credit translates into green investment. `No interaction detected' is not the same as `no complementarity exists'. A linear interaction may miss a threshold effect, given that SBFN's framework is graded and our coding is binary, and the WGI may not capture banking-supervisory capacity. We consider our reading the best supported by the evidence, but it does not rule these alternatives out.

The one nominally significant interaction, a negative overdraft $\times$ SBFN term, is at least consistent with guidelines steering credit towards term lending rather than towards working capital, although it does not survive multiple-testing correction. More generally, if smaller firms cannot absorb the fixed costs of green investment even when financed, a guideline aimed at bank lending acts on the wrong margin however credible it is.

\subsection{Lessons for policy and for evaluation}
\label{sec:lessons}

First, monitoring adoption is not monitoring effect. SBFN's progress framework scores members on the guidelines they issue and the stage they reach, and on our evidence being further along it does not coincide with any change in the firm-level margin the guidelines target. Programmes of this kind need outcome indicators alongside output indicators, at a minimum the share of bank credit financing green investment and firm-level adoption measured before and after a guideline takes effect.

Second, the data needed for a proper firm-level evaluation do not yet exist. Re-fielding a comparable Green Economy module in the same economies would allow the within-country before-and-after comparison this paper could run only at country level, and would let future evaluations locate where in the theory of change the chain breaks.

Third, bank-side guidelines may need firm-side complements, such as technical assistance, matching grants or credit guarantees for smaller firms, if the binding constraint lies with firms rather than with banks. The firm-size evidence here points that way but is exploratory.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We evaluated SBFN green banking guidelines against their own theory of change. Bank credit is robustly associated with green-practice adoption, but that association is no stronger in adopting economies and does not depend on regulatory quality, on either of two independent firm-level samples and under four estimators. A staggered-adoption event study finds no improvement in adopters' aggregate environmental outcomes. The policy implication is not that green banking guidelines are ineffective in every respect, but that our evidence gives no support to treating them as a lever that widens the green-investment gap between financed and unfinanced firms.

The evaluation has limits. The firm-level samples are cross-sections and cannot rule out reverse causality or unobserved country characteristics. SBFN membership is a binary proxy for a graded process, and WGI regulatory quality is a broad governance measure rather than a supervisory one. The global sample's outcome is a single item. The design tests the intensive margin of the credit channel, not whether guidelines change who obtains credit, which our measure of realised access cannot observe, since some firms decline formal credit altogether \citep{jin2026}. And the event study, an aggregate test netted against one covariate, cannot exclude a small confounded relationship.
