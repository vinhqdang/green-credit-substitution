# Peer Review Report

**Manuscript:** "Credible on Paper? Green Banking Policy, Access to Finance, and Institutional Capacity in the Greening of Firms Worldwide"
**Reviewed for:** *Economic Systems* (Elsevier) — Special Issue, "Governing the Green Transition as System Change: State Capacity, Policy Credibility, and Institutional Complementarities in Emerging and Transition Economies" (guest editors: Yu Hao, Mariya Gubareva, Xunpeng (Roc) Shi; managing editor Jan-Egbert Sturm)
**Review mode:** `academic-paper-reviewer`, full mode (5-seat panel + editorial synthesis) + dedicated special-issue-fit assessment against the verbatim CFP
**Review basis:** `manuscript/*.md` (all sections), `latex/manuscript.tex`/`titlepage.tex`, `data/`, `src/` (verified sample sizes, estimators, and identification claims against the code where the text made a checkable claim)
**Constraint:** Read-only review. No changes made to the manuscript or code.

---

## Phase 0 — Field & Configuration Note

Field: applied microeconometrics / political economy of financial regulation, testing a firm-level finance→green-investment channel and its cross-level moderation by a country-level green-banking-policy variable (SBFN status) and a governance indicator (WGI Regulatory Quality). Paper type: original empirical research, two independent datasets, four estimators. Target-journal tier: field journal special issue with an explicit, unusually specific methodological screening bar (see CFP verbatim below). This shapes the panel: the Journal-Fit seat is weighted heavily toward the CFP's own stated screening language rather than generic "is this a good paper" judgment, since the CFP is unusually explicit about what will fail initial editorial screening regardless of execution quality.

## Phase 1 — Panel Reviews

### Reviewer 1 (Journal-Fit / "EIC" seat)

**Recommendation signal:** Major revision — contingent primarily on the special-issue-fit question addressed in its own section below, not on the paper's technical execution, which is strong.

The paper is well matched to *Economic Systems* on subject matter (comparative institutions, financial-sector governance, emerging/transition economies) and to this special issue's stated topic list — it sits squarely under "institutional complementarities involving financial systems, credit allocation, bank regulation... and transition finance." The two-sample design (41-economy ECA/MENA primary sample, independently replicated on a 162-economy global sample) is unusually strong external-validity evidence for a paper of this type, and the honest, prominently reported null on H2/H3 — rather than a p-hacked positive interaction — is a genuine strength that many submissions in this literature do not offer.

The core tension for journal fit is that the CFP's own stated screening bar is narrower than "is this a good institutional-economics paper": it names specific *identification strategies* (DID/event studies, RDD, synthetic control, spatial/network designs, dose-response, local projections, structural approaches) and explicitly warns that submissions "without a clear identification strategy are unlikely to be considered." This paper's four estimators (pooled logit, Bayesian hierarchical/multilevel, causal forest DML, country-FE logit) are all methodologically sophisticated, but none of them is drawn from, or functionally equivalent to, the list the CFP names — they are estimators for recovering a conditional association under a selection-on-observables (unconfoundedness) assumption, not designs that exploit exogenous timing, discontinuities, or synthetic counterfactuals in *policy adoption itself*. See the dedicated Special Issue Fit section below for the full analysis and a concrete path to closing this gap before submission.

### Reviewer 2 (Methodology)

**Recommendation signal:** Minor-to-moderate revision on execution; one structural point escalated below.

Strengths: the four-estimator escalation is genuinely well-motivated rather than a checklist exercise — Stage 1 is explicitly reported to *demonstrate* why country FE cannot identify H2/H3 in the primary sample rather than to claim a result from it, which is an unusually transparent piece of econometric exposition. The Bayesian hierarchical model's diagnostics (all r-hat ≤ 1.01, ess_bulk 797–2,270) are reported to a standard that exceeds most applied papers in this literature. The causal forest's feature-importance decomposition (firm size 75.6%, regulatory quality 19.9%, SBFN 2.6%) is a genuinely novel finding rather than a restatement of the interaction-coefficient null, and the paper is commendably honest that the SME/large-firm coarse-strata split (Table 6b) does *not* recover a monotonic gradient consistent with that importance share — many authors would have quietly smoothed over that inconsistency.

Concerns, in order of severity:

1. **Endogenous SBFN adoption / country selection (unaddressed).** The identification of H2/H3 rests on comparing firms in SBFN-adopting vs. non-adopting economies. Nothing in the design addresses the obvious possibility that *which* economies adopt an SBFN framework is itself correlated with unobserved country characteristics that also shape the baseline finance-green relationship (e.g., donor/IFC engagement intensity, banking-sector development trajectory, prior environmental-policy ambition). Section 6's own Limitation 1 acknowledges this ("cannot rule out... unobserved, time-invariant country characteristics correlated with both SBFN adoption and the baseline propensity to adopt green practices") but the paper does not attempt any design that would mitigate it (e.g., matching adopters to non-adopters on pre-adoption observables, an event-study around each economy's own join year using whatever repeated WBES waves exist for a subset of countries, or at minimum a formal selection-on-observables sensitivity analysis, e.g. Oster's delta or an Altonji-Elder-Taber-style bounds exercise). Given that the entire H2/H3 apparatus is a moderation test on a country-level treatment that is not randomly assigned, this is the paper's central econometric vulnerability and it is currently handled entirely in prose, not in the design.

2. **Reverse causality on the credit-access treatment itself (also acknowledged, also unaddressed).** The same limitation section flags that firms already predisposed to green investment may find it easier to obtain credit. This is treated as an out-of-scope caveat throughout, including in the global-sample country-FE specification, which (as correctly stated) sharpens identification only for the *interaction* terms, not for the H1 main effect. Given H1 ("credit access → green adoption") is the paper's most emphasized headline number (13pp AME, repeated in the abstract, highlights, and conclusion), and it is the one claim in the paper *not* protected by any of the four escalating specifications, this asymmetry — a very cautious treatment of the null moderation result alongside a comparatively confident headline treatment of an equally endogenous main effect — should be corrected in the framing. Suggest explicitly labeling H1 as descriptive/associational throughout (it already is, but the prose sometimes reads more causally than the design supports, e.g., "a firm holding a bank credit line is roughly 13 percentage points more likely...").

3. **Country-clustered SEs on n≈41 clusters (Stage 1).** Correctly flagged as a motivation for Stage 2, but Stage 1's own reported baseline coefficients (Table 4) still get referenced elsewhere in the paper (e.g., the AME repeated in the abstract) without a caveat about small-cluster inference. Consider a wild cluster bootstrap on the Stage-1 baseline as a robustness footnote, since that number is the one most quoted outside the paper's own hierarchical corrective.

4. **Multiple-comparisons exposure on the one significant interaction.** The paper is admirably self-aware about this (Section 5.6, Section 6: "a single significant coefficient among the many interaction tests reported across Tables 4-8") — this is good practice, not a defect, but the Discussion's loan-tenor interpretation of that one coefficient is given more narrative real estate (a full paragraph plus a paragraph in the Conclusion) than its evidentiary weight supports. Recommend either shortening this interpretation or explicitly computing a multiple-testing-adjusted p-value/false-discovery-rate across the full set of interaction terms tested (Tables 4, 5, 6, 8, 9) to let the reader judge how surprising that one hit really is.

5. **Causal forest external validity of the "causal" label.** The causal forest/DML estimates (Section 5.4) are described in text as if straightforwardly causal ("the estimated effect of credit access... CATEs"), but the underlying identifying assumption (unconfoundedness given the observed covariate set — size, sector, SBFN, regulatory quality) is exactly as strong an assumption as the logit's, just nonparametrically flexible rather than linear. The paper would be more precise calling these "doubly-robust conditional associations under unconfoundedness" in at least one place, since a reader skimming for "causal forest = causal identification" is likely to over-read the rigor this buys relative to Stage 1.

6. **Minor: apparent broken cross-reference.** Section 6 (Discussion, Limitation 1) states "Section 2 discusses why a genuine panel on this outcome does not exist in the published WBES data" — Section 2 (Institutional background) as currently drafted contains no such discussion; it is entirely about the SBFN's history and progression framework. Either this is a stale cross-reference to content that was moved/cut, or the discussion needs to be added to Section 2 (or Section 4) as promised. Worth a copyedit pass regardless of the special-issue-fit outcome.

### Reviewer 3 (Domain)

**Recommendation signal:** Minor revision.

Literature coverage is genuinely strong for this specific niche: the paper correctly situates itself between the China-centric green-credit-guideline literature (Li et al. 2023; Huang, Gao, and Jia 2023; Dai et al. 2025) and the broader finance-constraint-and-green-investment literature (Ghosh and Dutta 2022; De Haas et al. 2025; Costa et al. 2024), and it is careful and correct to flag that the existing supporting evidence for an SBFN-style moderation effect is "drawn from single-country studies of a single, unusually well-resourced green-credit regime" (Section 3.2) — this framing of the gap is exactly right and is the paper's strongest contribution claim. The explicit engagement with Hall and Soskice (2001) varieties-of-capitalism and the La Porta et al. (1998)/Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson (2001) institutional-complementarity lineage in Section 3.3 is appropriately pitched for this special issue's theoretical register, and the paper visibly makes an effort to cite recent *Economic Systems*-adjacent work (Barra and Falcone 2026; Heyert and Weill 2025; Bach, Le, Luong, and Vu 2025; Horvath, Horvatova, and Siranova 2025) — a good-faith signal of engagement with the target venue's own recent output.

Two domain-level concerns:

1. The theoretical mechanism connecting H3 (regulatory quality moderates H2) to the varieties-of-capitalism/state-capacity literature is asserted more than derived. The paper states the institutional-complementarity logic clearly in prose (Section 3.3) but does not derive a testable functional form beyond "regulatory quality increasing" — a single linear interaction term is, in the end, a fairly blunt instrument for testing an institutional-complementarity claim of this theoretical pedigree, and the null result (Section 5.3, 5.7) is consistent with several distinct underlying stories (no complementarity; a nonlinear/threshold complementarity that a linear interaction cannot detect; a complementarity that operates through a channel WGI Regulatory Quality does not proxy well, as the paper itself concedes in Limitation 5). The paper would benefit from a paragraph distinguishing these possibilities more sharply, since "no interaction found" and "no complementarity exists" are not identical claims and the Discussion sometimes elides them.

2. The paper's own most novel empirical finding — that effect heterogeneity is overwhelmingly firm-size-driven rather than institutional (Section 5.4) — is arguably under-theorized relative to how much analytical weight it carries in the Discussion and Conclusion. The "firm-level absorptive capacity" reframing in Section 6 is a good instinct but is introduced late and given only prose treatment; it would strengthen the paper's contribution claim (and, not incidentally, its fit to this special issue — see below) to develop this as a fourth hypothesis with its own brief theoretical grounding (e.g., financing-constraint/fixed-cost literature: Ullah 2025 is already cited and directly on point) rather than as a post-hoc redirection introduced in the Discussion.

### Reviewer 4 (Perspective / cross-disciplinary & policy)

**Recommendation signal:** Minor revision.

The policy-relevance framing is a genuine strength and directly serves this special issue's political-economy orientation: the Conclusion's policy implication ("focus supervisory attention on term-lending instruments... and complementary measures... that address smaller firms' absorptive-capacity constraints directly, rather than assuming a bank-level supervisory mandate alone will reach firms regardless of scale") is a concrete, falsifiable, and non-obvious policy recommendation — exactly the kind of "so what" a political-economy special issue wants, and it follows honestly from a null result rather than overclaiming from it.

Two suggestions from a policy/practitioner-facing perspective:

1. The paper would benefit from directly addressing the SBFN's own supervisory stakeholders (banking regulators, the IFC as network convener) with a short paragraph on what this null result implies for the framework's own self-evaluation practice, given the IFC/SBFN periodically publishes Progress Reports that measure adoption stage but (per the paper's own account) not firm-level outcomes — this paper is arguably the first firm-level outcome check on the network's own theory of change, which is a stakeholder-relevant framing not yet made explicit.

2. Cross-disciinary connection to the just-transition/distributional literature (one of the CFP's own listed topics) is entirely absent. Given the firm-size heterogeneity finding, a brief connection to the SME-financing/just-transition literature (who is left behind when green-credit policy operates on an extensive banking margin rather than a firm-capacity margin) would strengthen both the paper's contribution and, again, its topical alignment with this specific CFP.

### Reviewer 5 (Devil's Advocate)

**Strongest counter-argument (required, cannot be omitted):**

The paper's four-estimator, two-sample design is real and well-executed, but all four estimators answer the *same underlying question* — "conditional on observables, does the credit–green association differ by SBFN status/regulatory quality?" — under the *same underlying identifying assumption* (unconfoundedness / selection on observables), applied at increasing levels of methodological sophistication. Sophistication of estimator is not the same axis as strength of identification. A Bayesian hierarchical model and a causal forest are both, at bottom, more flexible ways of conditioning on the same observed covariates as the Stage-1 logit; neither exploits an exogenous source of variation in *policy adoption timing*, a *discontinuity*, a *synthetic control*, or an *instrument*. This matters specifically because this special issue's CFP names exactly those design classes and explicitly distinguishes them from "purely descriptive analyses... without a clear identification strategy." A skeptical reading of this paper is: this is a very well-executed *robustness exercise* around a single cross-sectional association (does the null on the moderator survive four different flexible functional forms and independent replication?), not a paper that identifies and tests an institutional mechanism using a design built to isolate that mechanism causally. The paper's own honesty about this (Limitation 1, and Stage 1's explicit "we report this to make the limitation visible") is a mitigating factor — this is not a paper hiding a weak design — but honesty about an identification gap is not the same as closing it, and this special issue's editorial screening is explicitly pre-committed to screening on exactly this gap regardless of authors' candor about it.

**Issue list:**

- **CRITICAL** — [Special-issue fit / identification] The paper's strongest and most repeated evidentiary claim (replication across two independent samples with different outcomes, periods, and country sets) establishes *robustness of an association*, not *causal identification of a mechanism*. If the guest editors' initial screening applies the CFP's stated bar literally ("submissions... that lack a clear institutional, governance, or system-level mechanism are unlikely to be considered... purely descriptive analyses or studies without a clear identification strategy are unlikely to be considered"), this paper is at meaningful risk of failing that screen before technical peer review begins, regardless of its executed quality. See Special Issue Fit section for adjudication and a concrete mitigation path.
- **MAJOR** — [Alternative explanation not ruled out] A null credit×SBFN interaction is consistent with at least three distinct stories the paper does not fully distinguish: (a) SBFN policy genuinely does not affect the finance-green channel (the paper's preferred reading); (b) SBFN policy affects the channel but through banks' *supply-side* behavior in a way not captured by whether the *individual firm* already has a credit line (i.e., the policy could change *who* gets credit and on what terms, effects fully absorbed into the treatment variable itself rather than visible as a treatment-effect interaction); (c) SBFN adoption itself is confounded with country characteristics that suppress the very interaction it would otherwise produce (Reviewer 2's endogeneity point). The paper leans on (a) throughout the Discussion without giving (b) serious engagement, even though (b) is arguably the more theoretically interesting "institutional mechanism" story a system-change-oriented special issue would want addressed: the guideline could be working exactly as intended on the extensive margin of credit allocation, in which case testing its effect only among firms that already cleared that margin would mechanically produce a null on the interaction term regardless of the policy's true effect.
- **MINOR** — [Overgeneralization risk in framing] The phrase "green banking policy's binding constraint operates through firm-level absorptive capacity rather than... country-level regulatory capacity" (Abstract, Discussion) is stated as though firmly established, but rests on a single, honestly-reported inconsistency (75.6% importance share vs. no monotonic size gradient in the coarse-strata cut, Section 5.4) that the authors themselves say they "cannot yet" resolve. Recommend softening this specific sentence in the Abstract to match the more careful hedging already present in Section 5.4 and Section 6.
- **Ignored alternative path:** a difference-in-differences or event-study design around each economy's own SBFN join year, using whatever repeated (even if not identical-instrument) WBES waves exist for the same countries across years, is never attempted or explicitly ruled out with a data-availability citation in the main text (only obliquely, via the dangling Section 2 cross-reference Reviewer 2 flagged). Given that the paper's own Section 2 documents SBFN join years spanning 2012–2024 with real cross-country variation in adoption timing, this is exactly the kind of staggered-adoption variation the CFP's methods section names first ("staggered DID/event studies") — its absence, or an explicit, well-cited statement of why it is infeasible with this data, is the single most consequential gap between this paper and the CFP's stated expectations.
- **Missing stakeholder perspective:** the paper is framed entirely from the researcher/policy-evaluator perspective; it does not consider how banks themselves (the actual agents whose lending decisions SBFN guidelines target) might rationally respond to a supervisory mandate in ways invisible to a firm-level credit-access/green-adoption cross-tab (e.g., relabeling existing loans as "green," a compliance response documented in the green-taxonomy/greenwashing literature that this paper does not cite or rule out).

**Observations (non-defects):** The paper's transparency about its own null result, its explicit demonstration of *why* Stage 1 cannot identify H2/H3 rather than silently running a misspecified model, and its willingness to report an inconsistency in its own most novel finding (Table 6b's non-monotonic size gradient) are all unusually high-integrity choices for applied work under submission pressure, and should be preserved rather than smoothed over in any revision.

## Phase 2 — Editorial Synthesis

**Consensus across seats:** All five seats agree the paper is technically well-executed, honest about its own limitations, and a genuine, non-trivial contribution to the green-credit-policy literature specifically (the first firm-level, cross-national test of SBFN-style moderation, independently replicated). All five seats also converge, independently, on the same structural concern from different angles: Reviewer 1 (journal fit), Reviewer 2 (methodology: endogenous adoption/reverse causality), and the Devil's Advocate (CRITICAL: identification-strategy mismatch with the CFP) all identify the same underlying issue — the paper's evidentiary strategy is "robustness of an association across estimators and samples," not "causal identification of an institutional mechanism" in the sense this specific CFP names. This is not a case of one dissenting seat; it is corroborated from three independent angles, which raises its weight in this synthesis per the panel's evidence standards.

**Devil's Advocate CRITICAL adjudication (required, cannot be silently bypassed):** The CRITICAL finding above (special-issue identification-strategy fit) is adjudicated as **validated** — it is independently corroborated by Reviewer 1 and Reviewer 2's methodology point, and it tracks the CFP's own verbatim language rather than a general reviewer preference. It does not, by itself, mean the underlying empirical work is wrong or unpublishable — the panel's overall read is that this is solid, honest applied econometrics — but it is a real and specific risk for *this particular venue's stated screening criterion*, and is treated as the paper's single most consequential open issue for this submission decision.

**Editorial decision (general journal standard, if this were a regular submission to *Economic Systems*):** Minor-to-Major Revision. The empirical work is sound; the requested revisions (endogeneity/selection discussion strengthened into an actual sensitivity analysis, H1 causal language softened, multiple-testing treatment of the one significant interaction, the dangling Section 2 cross-reference, sharper theoretical grounding of the firm-size-heterogeneity finding) are all addressable without new data collection.

**Editorial decision (this special issue specifically):** See below — this is a distinct judgment from the general-journal decision above, because the CFP applies a screening criterion this paper does not straightforwardly satisfy on its face, independent of the quality of its execution.

## Special Issue Fit — Verdict and Recommendations

### Verdict: **Marginal fit — real risk of failing initial editorial screening on identification-strategy grounds, despite strong topical fit and strong execution.**

**Where the paper fits the CFP well:**

- **Multi-country scope.** Directly satisfies "demonstrate relevance beyond a single country or case" — more than satisfies it, in fact (41 and then 162 economies, independently).
- **Topical match.** Sits squarely inside the listed topic "Institutional complementarities involving financial systems, credit allocation, bank regulation... and transition finance," and secondarily touches "policy credibility" (the paper's own framing — "credible on paper?" is literally the working title) and "state capacity" (regulatory quality as a capacity proxy).
- **Political-economy framing.** The paper explicitly engages the CFP's own organizing question — "why do similar climate and energy policies generate systematically different outcomes across countries... and how do institutional and governance structures shape these outcomes" — almost verbatim in its own framing (Section 3.3, Section 6).
- **Not purely descriptive.** The paper is not a descriptive case study; it estimates a formal moderation model across four estimators with proper uncertainty quantification. This distinguishes it from the CFP's most clearly excluded category (single-country average-effect studies with no mechanism test at all).

**Where the fit is genuinely at risk:**

- **Identification strategy.** The CFP names specific design classes (staggered DID/event studies, RDD, synthetic control, spatial/network designs, dose-response, local projections, macro-financial evidence, mechanism-consistent structural approaches) as its bar for "credible identification strategy," and warns explicitly that studies without one are "unlikely to be considered" *regardless of whether they test an institutional variable at all*. This paper's four estimators are escalating in modeling sophistication, not in causal identification strength — none of them isolates exogenous variation in SBFN policy adoption itself. A guest editor or screening reviewer applying the CFP language literally could reasonably read this paper as "a well-executed cross-sectional/hierarchical association study with a null moderation result," which is precisely the profile the CFP pre-commits to screening out, even though the underlying research question (does state capacity/policy credibility condition a financial-institutional effect?) is exactly on-topic.
- **"Mechanism" framing.** The CFP wants papers to "explicitly identify and empirically test institutional or systemic mechanisms," not only test whether an institutional variable's *interaction coefficient* is significant. As the Devil's Advocate and Domain reviewer both note, a single linear interaction term tested for significance is a narrower operationalization of "mechanism" than the CFP's Hall-and-Soskice-adjacent framing implies, and the paper's most mechanism-like contribution (firm-level absorptive capacity as the real binding constraint, Section 5.4/6) is currently a secondary, under-theorized finding rather than the paper's designed-in test.

### Concrete recommendations before submission (in priority order)

1. **Address the staggered-adoption opportunity explicitly, one way or the other.** SBFN join years in this paper's own data span 2012–2024 (Section 2). Either (a) determine whether enough WBES-country-years with *some* comparable green-practice or finance-access measure exist across multiple survey waves per country to mount even a partial event-study/staggered-DID check around each economy's own adoption date (this would directly and literally answer the CFP's named method), or (b) if genuinely infeasible (the dangling Section 2 cross-reference suggests the authors already worked this out and the text just didn't survive an edit — this should be recovered and stated explicitly, with citation to the specific data limitation, in the main methodology section rather than left as a stray reference), make that infeasibility argument itself a first-class, prominent part of Section 4.3 and the cover letter, not a buried limitation. Editors screening against their own CFP will be far more receptive to "here is why the CFP's named designs are not available for this specific question and data, and here is our best alternative" stated up front than to discovering the gap themselves.
2. **Reframe the contribution, in the abstract and introduction, around the mechanism test rather than the null-interaction result per se.** Lead with the firm-level-absorptive-capacity finding (Section 5.4) as the paper's answer to "what mechanism governs whether this policy type works," rather than presenting it as a secondary redirection discovered after reporting a null. This is both better science communication and a more direct answer to what this CFP is actually asking for ("Policy Design × System Conditions → System Outcomes" — the firm-size finding *is* a system-conditions-shape-outcomes finding; the current framing undersells it as such).
3. **Add a formal selection/robustness treatment of SBFN-adoption endogeneity** (matching on pre-adoption observables, an Oster-delta-style coefficient-stability bound, or at minimum a clearly labeled sensitivity analysis), both because it strengthens the paper on its own econometric merits (Reviewer 2) and because it directly answers the "credible identification strategy" screening criterion with something more substantive than an escalating series of flexible-functional-form estimators sharing one identifying assumption.
4. **In the cover letter to the guest editors**, address the CFP's identification-strategy language directly and pre-emptively: state explicitly why the paper's own escalating four-estimator design (culminating in an independently-replicated, country-fixed-effects specification on a second, much larger sample) is the paper's answer to the credible-identification requirement given the specific data constraints of firm-level cross-country survey data on this outcome, rather than leaving the guest editors to make that inference themselves during initial screening.

If items 1 and 4 above are not addressed, the panel's honest assessment is that this manuscript — despite being a genuinely strong, honest piece of applied econometrics that would likely fare well at *Economic Systems* as a regular (non-special-issue) submission — carries a real risk of not clearing this special issue's own stated initial editorial screen, for reasons that are about identification-strategy framing and fit to this specific CFP rather than about the underlying research quality.
