## 1. Introduction

Since 2012, more than sixty central banks, banking regulators, and financial-sector regulatory bodies across the emerging and transition world have signed on to a coordinated effort to green their financial systems, adopting national guidelines that direct commercial banks to price environmental and climate risk into lending decisions and to channel credit toward cleaner production. On paper, this represents one of the most ambitious diffusions of environmental financial regulation outside the OECD: economies as different in income, banking-sector depth, and political regime as Bangladesh, Morocco, Mongolia, and Indonesia now report, to the International Finance Corporation's Sustainable Banking and Finance Network (SBFN), formal sustainable-finance roadmaps, green-taxonomy pilots, or mandatory environmental-risk disclosure rules for lenders. Whether that adoption changes what actually happens on a factory floor — whether a firm that can get a bank loan is any more likely to install a wastewater treatment system, switch to a more efficient boiler, or measure its own emissions — is a separate and considerably harder question, and it is the one this paper answers.

The premise that formal adoption of a policy is not the same as its operation in practice is, of course, not new to institutional economics. A large literature on state capacity and policy credibility has long argued that the same law, regulation, or guideline can produce entirely different real-economy outcomes depending on the administrative and judicial infrastructure available to enforce it (La Porta et al., 1998; Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson, 2001). Applied to green finance specifically, a parallel body of work has documented that green credit guidelines in China measurably reshape lending allocation and firm environmental performance (Li, Feng, Li, and Zhou, 2023; Huang, Gao, and Jia, 2023; Dai, He, Guo, Zheng, and Zhang, 2025), that firms' green investment responds to the financing constraints they face (Ghosh and Dutta, 2022; Costa, Demmou, Franco, and Lamp, 2024; De Haas, Martin, Muûls, and Schweiger, 2025), and that the World Bank Enterprise Surveys' own Green Economy module is a workable lens on firm-level environmental behaviour across dozens of countries at once (Asif, Dhamija, Khan, Johri, and Wasiq, 2025; Phan, 2026). What is missing is the connection between these two literatures: a direct test, at firm level and across a wide cross-section of the very economies the green-banking-policy movement targets, of whether the credit-to-green-investment channel that these guidelines are designed to activate is actually stronger where the guidelines exist — and whether that depends on the state capacity to make the guideline credible in the first place.

This paper closes that gap using firm-level data from two independently assembled samples of World Bank Enterprise Survey (WBES) waves. The primary sample covers 47 country-years fielded between 2018 and 2023 that carry the Bank's full Green Economy module — a battery of questions on emissions monitoring, energy audits, and the adoption of climate-friendly production measures — alongside the Surveys' standard access-to-finance module; a second, global sample extends this to 162 further country-years fielded between 2021 and 2026, reaching for the first time in this literature into high-income economies with no SBFN involvement at all (Section 4). We match each economy to its Sustainable Banking and Finance Network policy status as of its survey year, distinguishing genuine ex-ante policy adopters from economies that joined only afterward, and to the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators as a measure of the regulatory state capacity available to make that policy credible. Econometrically, we depart deliberately from the pooled fixed-effects specification that has become the default in this literature. Because SBFN status is fixed within country in a single-wave cross-section, a country fixed effect absorbs it entirely, and a naive interaction term estimated by pooled logit — which we report first, as a baseline, precisely to make this limitation visible rather than to hide it — returns no detectable policy-moderation effect at all. We show that this null result is not evidence of an absent relationship so much as a symptom of forcing a fundamentally hierarchical question (does a level-2, country-level policy variable reshape a level-1, firm-level financing relationship?) into a flat, single-level model with too few country clusters to lean on for inference (Cameron and Miller, 2015). We then re-estimate the same relationship with tools built for exactly this structure: a Bayesian hierarchical logistic model with country-varying slopes (Gelman and Hill, 2007; Gelman, Hill, and Vehtari, 2020), a causal-forest / double-machine-learning (DML) estimator (Wager and Athey, 2018; Chernozhukov et al., 2018; Athey, Tibshirani, and Wager, 2019; Athey and Imbens, 2019) that lets the treatment effect of credit access vary flexibly across the institutional landscape rather than through a single estimated coefficient, and, on the much larger global sample, a country-fixed-effects specification that the primary sample's single wave cannot support.

This paper contributes to the literature in six respects. First, it provides what is, to our knowledge, the first direct empirical test of whether SBFN-style green banking policy adoption changes the firm-level relationship between finance and green investment, rather than only the aggregate volume of green lending reported by banks themselves — a firm-level, demand-side complement to the bank-level, supply-side evidence that dominates the existing green-credit-policy literature. Second, it extends the World Bank Enterprise Survey's Green Economy module — used until now mostly within single countries or single regional waves — into a harmonized 41-country primary sample spanning Europe, Central Asia, the Caucasus, and the Middle East and North Africa, giving the paper a breadth of institutional variation that a single-country or single-region design cannot offer. Third, and unusually for this literature, we subject the primary sample's central finding to an independently assembled second test: a 162-economy global sample built from a World Bank standardized cross-country database spanning 2021-2026 that, to our knowledge, no published study of green banking policy has yet exploited, extending coverage for the first time into high-income economies entirely outside the SBFN's own remit and allowing a country-fixed-effects specification the primary sample's single-wave design cannot support. That the same qualitative result — a robust finance-green channel, no detectable institutional moderation — survives this independent replication is, we think, considerably more informative than either sample could be alone. Fourth, methodologically, the paper demonstrates — with the same underlying question tested twice, on two different samples, under four different estimators — exactly where and why a classical fixed-effects approach fails to detect (or, when country fixed effects are usable, correctly fails to find) an institutionally-conditioned effect, offering applied researchers working with similarly structured cross-country firm data a concrete illustration of when the extra modelling investment is worth making and when a null result is simply a null result. Fifth, the paper redirects the question of which system-level margin governs green-credit-policy effectiveness: having ruled out a country-institutional interaction across four estimators and two samples, we show that the effect heterogeneity that does exist is concentrated at the level of firm size rather than country institutions, relocating the operative "system condition" in the Policy Design x System Conditions -> System Outcomes logic this special issue organizes itself around from the country-regulatory level to the firm-scale level — a mechanism we introduce as an auxiliary, exploratory hypothesis in Section 3.4 and demonstrate empirically in Section 5.4 rather than treat as a pre-registered finding, and are correspondingly explicit about the identification limits (Section 4) that a purely cross-sectional design, however extensively replicated, still carries. Sixth, and directly in response to that identification-strategy limit, Section 6 builds an entirely independent country-year panel from World Bank World Development Indicators and estimates a Callaway-Sant'Anna staggered event-study exploiting SBFN's own adoption-timing variation -- the design class this special issue's methodological guidance names first -- and finds that it, too, does not support a beneficial institutional effect on aggregate environmental outcomes, converging with the firm-level evidence from a design built specifically to meet an identification bar the firm-level data cannot.

The paper also speaks directly to a growing body of recent scholarship in this journal on the institutional and financial determinants of firm and bank behaviour in emerging and transition economies. Yan, Jia, and Zhang (2024) study how environmental protection taxes reshape green productivity among Chinese listed firms; Barra and Falcone (2026) examine how institutional factors condition environmental performance across a global sample of economies; Heyert and Weill (2025) and Bach, Le, Luong, and Vu (2025) use firm- and bank-level data across dozens of countries to study, respectively, trust-driven financial inclusion and credit constraints' effect on corporate tax behaviour in Vietnamese firms; Li, Liu, Li, and Gao (2026) study how shadow-banking engagement reshapes trade-credit financing among Chinese non-financial firms; and Horvath, Horvatova, and Siranova (2025) apply Bayesian model averaging to the cross-country determinants of financial development, a methodological precedent we build on directly in adopting a Bayesian estimation strategy in Section 4. This paper sits at the intersection of these threads — institutions, green performance, and firm-level financing behaviour, studied jointly rather than pairwise — and, to our knowledge, is the first among them to test whether a specific, dateable policy-adoption event (SBFN membership) moderates the finance-green relationship at firm level across a comparably broad cross-section of adopting economies.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 sets out the institutional background of the SBFN framework and the concepts and trends in green banking policy adoption across our sample. Section 3 develops the paper's three hypotheses from the state-capacity and institutional-complementarity literature. Section 4 describes the primary and global samples, variable construction, and the empirical strategy for each. Section 5 presents baseline, hierarchical, and heterogeneous-effect results on the primary sample, followed by the independent global-sample replication. Section 6 discusses the findings' economic magnitude and their implications for how green credit policy is designed and sequenced, and Section 7 concludes.
